# Crossing the Border at Huu Nghi: The Complete Land Travel Guide from China to Vietnam (2026)

*Photo: The main gate of Hữu Nghị Quan in Vietnam, by TSVC1190, CC BY-SA 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons.*

Huu Nghi is the busiest road crossing on the Vietnam–China frontier. It sits **north of Lang
Son city**, on the road corridor running up from Hanoi towards the border and Pingxiang on the
Chinese side, and it handles both freight and passenger traffic.

It is also the crossing where the single most expensive travel mistake in this guide happens —
and it happens weeks before anyone reaches the gate.

---

## 1. The Rule That Decides Your Trip

A Vietnam eVisa is issued **for a specific port of entry**, chosen by you at the moment of
application. That choice is binding.

At airports, officers occasionally show discretion when a traveller arrives at a different
airport than the one declared. **At land borders they do not.**

If your eVisa names Noi Bai, or Tan Son Nhat, or any airport at all, **you cannot use it to
walk across at Huu Nghi**. There is no counter at the gate that will amend it. An approved
eVisa cannot be edited. A mismatch means reapplying with the correct port — a second
application, a second wait, and a second non-refundable fee, while you stand on the wrong side
of a border.

**So: decide how you are entering Vietnam before you apply, not afterwards.** If your route
comes overland from Guangxi, select **Huu Nghi** on the application itself.

This is the whole guide in one paragraph. Everything below is detail.

## 2. Where It Is and How People Reach It

| | |
|---|---|
| **Vietnamese side** | Huu Nghi International Border Gate, north of Lang Son city |
| **Chinese side** | Pingxiang, Guangxi |
| **Approach from Vietnam** | By road from Hanoi, up the northern corridor through Lang Son |
| **Approach from China** | By road from Guangxi, via Pingxiang |
| **Traffic** | Both freight and passengers; the busiest road crossing on this frontier |

Most independent travellers approach from Hanoi by road, or from the Chinese side through
Guangxi. Overland transport on both sides is straightforward to arrange locally.

One realistic expectation to set: **overland travel is considerably less predictable than a
flight.** Border formalities, freight queues and connecting transport all introduce
variability that a flight schedule does not have. Build slack into anything you book for the
same day — and be sceptical of an itinerary that has you crossing at Huu Nghi in the morning
and catching something fixed in Hanoi that afternoon.

## 3. What to Have Ready

| Item | Why it matters here specifically |
|---|---|
| **Passport valid 6+ months** from your arrival date | Standard entry condition, checked at the gate |
| **eVisa naming Huu Nghi**, printed on **A4** | Do not rely on a phone at a land border |
| **Proof of onward travel** out of Vietnam within your permitted stay | Officers may ask; carry a printed itinerary |
| **Cash** for the crossing day | Card acceptance at land borders is not something to depend on |

Two of those deserve emphasis because they are where land crossings differ from airports.

**Print everything.** Airports are used to travellers presenting a phone. Land gates are
paper-oriented, and a battery that dies in a queue is not an argument.

**A bus or train ticket is harder to present convincingly than a flight booking.** If your
onward plan is overland, carry a printed itinerary rather than expecting a screenshot of a
booking app to satisfy the question.

## 4. Customs: The Second Checkpoint

Clearing immigration is not clearing the border. Customs is a separate checkpoint, and the
limits apply **identically at a land gate and at an airport**.

The thresholds worth knowing before you pack:

- **Foreign currency exceeding US$5,000** (or equivalent) must be declared
- **Vietnamese dong exceeding 15,000,000 VND** must be declared

Duty-free personal allowances:

- Spirits over 20% ABV: up to **1.5 litres** — *or* wine under 20% ABV: up to **2.0 litres** —
  *or* beer: up to **3.0 litres**
- Tobacco: **200 cigarettes** *or* **250 grams** of loose tobacco

Restricted items that cause real problems at land gates: **drones may require permits or
approval**, weapons and prohibited goods are exactly what they sound like, and fresh meat or
animal products may require quarantine.

Declaring takes a few minutes. Being found not to have declared does not.

## 5. Sequence at the Gate

1. **Complete exit formalities** on the Chinese side at Pingxiang.
2. **Cross to the Vietnamese post** at Huu Nghi.
3. **Present passport and printed eVisa** at immigration. The port on the visa must read
   Huu Nghi.
4. **Clear customs** — green channel or red channel, using the limits above.
5. **Arrange onward transport** to Lang Son or Hanoi.

Expect the crossing to take longer when a bus arrives at the same time you do. Freight traffic
and passenger traffic share the corridor.

## 6. Timing Your Application

Standard eVisa processing runs about **3 to 5 business days**, and weekends and Vietnamese
public holidays do not count toward that. An application submitted on a Friday afternoon does
not begin review until Monday morning.

For an overland crossing this matters more than it does for a flight, because your travel plan
is usually less flexible than an airline booking — you may already be in Guangxi when you
realise something is wrong, and fixing a port-of-entry error from there means waiting where
you are.

The fees are **US$25 for single entry** and **US$50 for multiple entry**, non-refundable if
refused. If you plan to leave Vietnam and return — a common pattern for travellers looping
through the region — a single-entry visa ends the moment you exit, so choose accordingly.

## 7. Frequently Asked Questions

**Can I cross at Huu Nghi with an eVisa that names an airport?**
No. Land borders have no flexibility on the declared port of entry. Reapply with Huu Nghi
selected.

**Can the port of entry be changed after approval?**
No. An approved eVisa cannot be edited. The only route is a new application.

**Do I need a printed copy of the eVisa?**
Yes. Print it on A4 and carry it with the passport.

**Will they ask for onward travel proof?**
They may. Carry a printed itinerary showing you leave Vietnam within your permitted stay.

**Can I pay by card at the border?**
Do not plan on it. Carry cash for the crossing day.

**How long does the crossing take?**
It varies with traffic, and freight shares the corridor. Do not book a fixed commitment in
Hanoi for the same afternoon.

## 8. Why Overland Travellers Get Caught More Often Than Flyers

The port-of-entry rule applies to everyone, but overland travellers trip on it far more, and
the reason is the order in which people plan.

Air travellers book a flight first. The flight names an airport, the airport goes on the visa
application, and the two match almost by accident.

Overland travellers book transport last. They decide on a region, apply for the visa while the
route is still loose, pick whatever port looks plausible in the dropdown — and only later work
out that they are actually crossing at Huu Nghi. By then the visa is approved and unchangeable.

The fix is a sequencing habit rather than any special knowledge: **settle the crossing point
first, then apply.** If the route is genuinely undecided, wait until it is not.

## 9. Verify These Yourself Before You Go

Border arrangements are not static, and this crossing carries freight as well as passengers,
which means operational details change more often than they do at an airport. Before you
travel, confirm independently:

- That **Huu Nghi is still designated for eVisa entry** at the time you apply — the designated
  list is expanded and revised periodically
- **Current opening arrangements** on both sides, which are not always the same hours
- Whether your **nationality has any additional requirement** beyond the eVisa
- **Current customs thresholds**, which are the figures most likely to be updated quietly
- The state of **onward transport** from the Vietnamese side on the day you cross

Nothing in this guide substitutes for the official portal on the day you apply. Treat it as
orientation, and let the official source settle anything that matters.
